**High availability → Uptime** watches whether your services are up, keeps their uptime per day,
records incidents and publishes a **public status page** anyone can open without signing in.

The page has three tabs: **Monitors** (with the incidents still open above them), **Incident
history** and **Public status page**. **New incident** is at the top, whichever tab is open. The open
tab is part of the page's address (`?tab=history`, for example).

<Note>
  Not the same as [Status pages](/proxy/status-pages): those replace a site with a maintenance,
  moved or blocked page. Uptime only watches and reports; it never changes what visitors of a site
  get.
</Note>

## Monitors

A monitor checks one of two things:

| Kind | What it checks | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A URL** | DNZ requests it: the status code (200–399 passes unless you list codes such as `200,301-302`), optionally a keyword the answer must contain, and how long the answer took. Redirects are followed. `GET` or `HEAD`, and "accept any certificate" for self-signed servers. | A route of Traefik, Caddy or DNZ Proxy, by its hostname |
| **A DNZ Proxy service** | The servers behind a hostname a DNZ Proxy route serves, from the upstream health the nodes already report every 5 seconds (for a Split, the servers of the upstreams it splits over; for a Fallback, its main and fallback upstreams; for a Mirror, its main upstream). Up while every server is healthy, degraded while some are, down when none is or no node reported in the last 30 seconds. Nothing extra is sent. | Proxied services, server by server. Turn on the route's health check for the clearest answer |

Monitors watch your routes only: the URL's hostname must be one a route of your proxies serves. The
dialog lists the proxied names; other sites and addresses are refused.

Each monitor has an interval (20 seconds to an hour, 60 seconds by default), a timeout and how many
**failed checks in a row** make it **down** (2 by default). A single failure shows it **degraded**
until the next check; a URL monitor can also count answers slower than a limit as degraded.
**Paused** monitors aren't checked and keep their history.

The list shows each monitor's state and since when, 90 days of uptime bars (30 on a phone; hover a
bar for that day), its uptime over 30 days, and a line of its last 60 checks' response times with
failed checks in red. Open a monitor for its uptime over 24 hours, 7, 30 and 90 days, its last check
and error. **Check now** runs one check and shows what it saw without keeping it; **Test** in the
dialog does the same before you save.

Uptime is the share of checks the monitor wasn't down (degraded counts as up), the same way over
24 hours (the checks of the last 24 hours, by the hour) as over 7, 30 and 90 days (the days with
checks). A monitor without checks in that time shows **—**, not 0%, and so does a new one without any
checks of its own yet (on the public status page too, never 100%). For a monitor younger than the
window, its uptime is since the day it was made, and the public page says so ("100% uptime since
28 Oct"), as does the Uptime page (*since 28 Oct* under the 30-day number, and in the monitor's
details). Days follow the display time zone and are kept 400 days.

<Info>
  Checks run on one server: the cluster's leader, so each runs once and alerts go out once. A DNZ
  secondary following a primary checks nothing; monitors are the primary's, and the secondary is
  read-only for them. URL monitors are requested from that server, so the URL must be reachable
  from there.
</Info>

## Routes under attack protection

Under attack and the waiting room would stop DNZ's own checks (a check runs no script). DNZ signs
its checks for each DNZ Proxy serving the hostname, and the nodes let them through those two;
sign-in, the WAF and the firewall still apply.

## A group of routes

Rather than one monitor per route, watch a whole group from **Proxy → Routes**: **Add monitor → A
group of routes**, pick the group and the path requested on each hostname (`/` or `/health`). DNZ
keeps a monitor per hostname of the group's routes, named after it. A route that joins the group
gets its monitor within 30 seconds, and one that leaves loses it. Changing the watch (the group's
card above the monitors) changes all of its monitors; **Stop watching** removes them or keeps them
as monitors of their own.

## Incidents

When a monitor goes down, DNZ opens an incident ("*name* unavailable", status **investigating**)
with what the failing check saw, and resolves it when the monitor is back ("Checks pass again
after 4m 10s"). You can post updates to it in between.

You can also write incidents yourself, for what monitors can't see or for planned work: a title, a
status (**investigating**, **identified**, **monitoring**, **resolved**), an impact and the monitors
it affects, then updates over time. Each update sets the incident's status: resolved ends it,
another status opens it again.

| Impact | The affected monitors show on the public page as |
| --- | --- |
| Minor | Degraded performance |
| Major | Partial outage |
| Critical | Major outage |
| Maintenance | Under maintenance |

An incident that affects no monitor is about everything, and the page's overall status shows it.
An incident can be kept off the public page. Open incidents show above the monitors on the
**Monitors** tab; those resolved in the last 90 days are on the **Incident history** tab.
Both lists come a page at a time: the monitors with a search and a filter by state (up, degraded,
down, unknown, paused), the incident history newest first.

### Incidents written afterwards

A power cut takes DNZ down with everything else: its checks can't see the outage, and nobody can
write the incident until it is back. So an incident's times can be in the past, never in the future:
**Started** (default now), **Resolved** when you open it as resolved or edit it later, and each
update's **time** (default now). They go in order: started, then the updates, then resolved. The
newest update sets the incident's status; an update dated before it fills in the timeline and leaves
the status as it is. The fields are on the server's clock, the time zone of Settings → General,
shown beside them.

When DNZ was off for more than 5 minutes, the Uptime page offers it once it is back: *DNZ wasn't
checking from 13:04 to 14:31 — open an incident for it?* **Open incident** fills in both times;
**Dismiss** ends the offer.

Where DNZ checked, its checks count. Where it wasn't checking, an incident you wrote counts for the
monitors it names, as the checks they would have made then:

| Impact | Counts as |
| --- | --- |
| Critical, Major | Down |
| Minor | Degraded (up in the percentages) |
| Maintenance | Nothing (the bars show maintenance) |

That goes into the bars, the 24-hour, 7, 30 and 90-day uptime, the public page (for incidents shown
there) and the metrics. Hovering a bar says how many of its checks were filled in. Incidents DNZ
opened itself fill nothing, nor does time before a monitor was added.

### Scheduled maintenance

Maintenance planned under **Proxy → Status pages** keeps uptime quiet for the hostnames it covers:
a [repeating window](/proxy/status-pages#scheduled-maintenance), or a **maintenance**
[status page](/proxy/status-pages) that is on (also one set on
[several routes at once](/proxy/overview#several-routes-at-once)).

- A monitor of such a hostname (an HTTP monitor's URL host, a service monitor's hostname) keeps
  checking and keeping its results, but while the maintenance is on a failed check counts nothing:
  no failures in a row, no automatic incident, no alert, and nothing in its days or its uptime. Its
  row says *In maintenance until 23:00*. A monitor that was already down stays down, with its
  incident open. A monitor whose checks have all failed in maintenance is still *not checked yet*
  and shows **—** for its uptime, never 100%; a passing check counts, maintenance or not.
- At each occurrence of a repeating window that covers a monitor, DNZ opens an incident
  "Scheduled maintenance: *name*" (impact **Maintenance**, so those days show as maintenance) and
  resolves it with "Maintenance finished." at the end. It is on the public page when the window is
  set to be and one of its monitors is on the page.
- The public page lists the next 7 days' occurrences under **Scheduled maintenance**.
- A window with **Uptime stays quiet** off still shows its maintenance page, but monitors alert as
  usual.

<Tip>
  To take sites down for maintenance, set a status page on their routes rather than turning the
  routes off: a [watched group](#a-group-of-routes)'s monitors follow its routes that are on, so
  turning routes off removes their monitors.
</Tip>

## The public status page

Turn it on in the **Public status page** card, on the tab of the same name. It is at `http(s)://<your DNZ>/status`, its content
as JSON at `/status.json` ([its fields](/api/uptime#the-page-as-json), scheduled maintenance included), and on its own hostname if you give one. It shows:

- your logo and label from **Settings → Profile**, a title and description
- the overall status: all systems operational, degraded performance, partial or major outage, under maintenance
- open incidents with their timeline
- **Scheduled maintenance**: the coming 7 days' occurrences of [repeating maintenance](#scheduled-maintenance) that covers a monitor on the page
- the monitors you put on it, under the **public name** and **group** you give them, with their state, uptime bars and uptime
- past incidents of the last 14 days (1 to 90), under the days that had one (one line when there were none)

Visitors see only what you chose for them: public names, groups, incident titles and the updates
you write. Never a monitor's own name, URL, address, status code or error.

The page is one self-contained document (no fonts or scripts from elsewhere; the only outside image
is a logo given as an `https://` address), so it works over plain http and whatever else is down. It
reloads itself every minute and shows times in the visitor's time zone.

### On its own hostname

Give the page a hostname, such as `status.example.com`, and DNZ answers requests for that name with
the page alone: neither the DNZ dashboard nor its API is reachable under it. Under **How visitors
reach it**, pick one:

- **Through a proxy, set up by DNZ:** choose a proxy and DNZ's own address as the proxy reaches it:
  its IP and HTTP port, prefilled from **Settings → General → Server → Device address**. Never the page's
  hostname (visitors would go round in a loop), and not `localhost` unless the proxy runs on the
  same machine. **Save** with the page on and DNZ makes the route (HTTPS, with the redirect from
  http) and the DNS record right away. Turning the page off deletes them again; a record that was
  there before only leaves the proxy. A wildcard route of the proxy (`*.example.com`) that sends
  other names elsewhere doesn't get in the way: the page's own route goes before it. The card shows
  the name's DNS records and routes as they are.
- **I'll point it at DNZ myself:** a DNS record pointing the name straight at DNZ (when DNZ's HTTP
  port is 80), or a route on a proxy DNZ doesn't manage, which must pass the visitor's hostname on.

```bash
dnz uptime page set --on --hostname status.example.com --proxy edge --dnz-address http://10.0.0.53:8080
```

Making or deleting the route needs **Manage proxies** too. If the route or DNS record can't be made,
the page is still saved and the card says why.

<Info>
  Visitors don't load DNZ: on its own hostname the page may be cached (30 s on DNZ Proxy, whose route
  DNZ makes with its cache on, 15 s in browsers), and DNZ builds it at most once every 10 seconds.
</Info>

## Alerts

Add the event **Uptime** to a [notification provider](/integrations/notifications). DNZ notifies
when a monitor goes down, with what failed, and when it is up again, with how long it was down.
Webhooks get the monitor, state, error, status code, response time and incident in `details`.

## Prometheus

| Metric | Labels | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `dnz_uptime_up` | `monitor`, `kind` | 1 while up (or only degraded), 0 while down |
| `dnz_uptime_status` | `monitor`, `state` | 1 for the state it is in |
| `dnz_uptime_response_seconds` | `monitor` | How long its last check took (URL monitors) |
| `dnz_uptime_ratio_30d` | `monitor` | Share of the last 30 days' checks it was up |
| `dnz_uptime_last_check_timestamp_seconds` | `monitor` | When it was last checked |
| `dnz_uptime_open_incidents` | | Incidents not resolved yet |

The bundled alert rules include `DnzUptimeMonitorDown` (2 minutes down). See
[Prometheus](/dashboard/prometheus).

## From the terminal and assistants

`dnz uptime` does all of this from the terminal (see [Uptime CLI](/cli/uptime)); the
[API](/api/uptime) has the same. AI assistants connected over [MCP](/integrations/ai-assistants) can
read monitors and incidents with `list_uptime_monitors` (with the times DNZ wasn't checking) and `list_uptime_incidents`, and scheduled
maintenance with `maintenance_windows` (`dnz proxy maintenance` in the terminal).

Reading needs **Manage monitoring**, **Manage proxies** or **Post incidents**; changing needs
**Manage monitoring**. **Post incidents** (the built-in **Helpdesk** role) also opens, edits and
updates incidents, without touching monitors, the public page or anything else; deleting an
incident stays with **Manage monitoring**.
Every change is in the [audit log](/admin/audit-log).
