<sub>October 24, 2026 · DNZ team</sub>

It's a Tuesday morning. The helpdesk phone starts ringing: the patient portal is slow, then it
times out. The servers behind it are pinned at 100% CPU. Nothing was deployed. Something out there
is hammering the site.

This post is the playbook for that morning, using what [dnz-proxy](/proxy/dnz-proxy) already has:
analytics, a firewall, an under-attack challenge, a cache, rate limits and status pages. Every step
reaches every node within seconds, and none of them needs a restart.

<Note>
  Everything here assumes the site already goes through a dnz-proxy proxy. If it doesn't yet, see
  [Proxied records](/proxy/overview) and the [five-step setup](/blog/cloudflare-in-your-server-room#building-it-in-five-steps).
</Note>

## Before the bad day: five minutes of preparation

The calmest incident is the one you prepared for. These take a few minutes and cost nothing while
traffic is normal.

- **Set a GeoIP database.** On the proxy's **Settings** tab, set **GeoIP database URL** to an
  `.mmdb` file such as MaxMind GeoLite2 Country or DB-IP's IP to Country Lite. Without it, analytics
  can't show countries and firewall rules can't match on them.
- **Attach a firewall to the router now**, even with nothing in it but a **Log** rule. Adding rules
  later is then one edit, not a change to the router.
- **Create an Under attack middleware** and leave it unattached. On the day, you only have to pick it.
- **Put a cache in front of anything static**, with **Stale if error** on, so a struggling origin
  doesn't take the whole site down with it.
- **Run more than one node.** DNS answers with every healthy node, so the load is already spread,
  and you can take one out without an outage.

## Step 1: Look before you block

Open **Proxy → Analytics** and pick the proxy and the hostname. Within a few seconds you want to know
**what kind of attack this is**, because each kind has a different answer:

| What you see | What it usually means |
| --- | --- |
| Requests up tenfold, most of them from a handful of clients | A few machines, or a misbehaving script |
| Requests spread over thousands of clients, mostly one country | A botnet, or a scraper rotating addresses |
| One path dominates (`/login`, `/search?q=…`) | Someone found your most expensive page |
| Latency p99 climbing, origin errors (5xx) rising | Your origin is the bottleneck, not the proxy |
| A strange user agent at the top | A tool that doesn't bother to hide |

The **top paths, clients, countries and user agents** tell you most of it. Then open the **request
log** in its live view and watch a few seconds of real requests: the pattern is usually obvious once
you see it scroll by.

<Tip>
  Blocks, challenges and errors get ten times the usual log budget per node, so the request log stays
  useful exactly when the traffic is worst.
</Tip>

## Step 2: Cut what you can name

If the attack has a fingerprint (an address range, a user agent, a path, a country), block it. Open
the firewall middleware and add rules. The first rule that matches decides, so the order matters:

1. **Allow** your own network first, for example your office range with **in range**. Your team
   must never be locked out of their own site while fixing it.
2. **Block** the fingerprint: a client range, a user agent that **contains** the tool's name, a path
   that **starts with** `/search` with an odd query parameter, or a country you don't serve.
3. **Log** everything else, so you can see what the rules aren't catching.

A **Block** answers with 403 by default, or any status and message you give it, before the request
ever reaches your servers. Watch the analytics: the **blocked** count should climb while the origin's
load falls.

## Step 3: Challenge what you can't name

When the traffic comes from thousands of addresses that look like ordinary browsers, there is no
fingerprint to block. That's what the **Under attack** challenge is for.

Attach the Under attack middleware to the router, or use a **Challenge** action in a firewall rule
to aim it at part of the traffic (one country, one path, everything but your office). Visitors with
a real browser see a short page that solves a small proof of work, get a cookie, and carry on. Most
attack tools never run the page, so they never get the cookie.

A few things to get right:

- **Difficulty** is in bits, and each bit doubles the work. 16 takes well under a second for a
  person. Raise it a step at a time if the attack keeps up.
- **APIs and forms** get a 403 until a page has been visited, because they can't run the challenge.
  If your mobile app talks to the same hostname, scope the challenge to browser paths with a firewall
  rule instead of attaching it to the whole router.
- **Let your own bots through** by user agent or address: uptime monitors, payment callbacks, search
  engines you care about.

## Step 4: Keep the origin breathing

Blocking and challenging take the worst off. What gets through should cost your servers as little as
possible.

- **Cache.** With a **Cache** middleware on the router, repeated requests for the same page are
  answered by the nodes. Turn on **Stale while revalidate** so only one request at a time goes to the
  origin for an expired page, and **Stale if error** so visitors still get the last good copy while
  the origin fails. Answers carry `X-Cache: HIT | MISS | STALE | BYPASS`, and the analytics show the
  hit ratio rising.
- **Rate limit the expensive paths.** A **rateLimit** middleware on a router for `/login` or
  `/search` caps each client. Counts are per node in memory, or shared through Redis so a client
  can't get around the limit by landing on another node. *One client is* can be the address, a
  header, or the hostname.
- **Cap concurrency.** **inFlightReq** limits how many requests a client may have open at once, and a
  **circuitBreaker** stops sending to an origin that is clearly failing, so it gets a moment to
  recover.

Middlewares are set up under the proxy's **Middlewares** tab and attached in the router's form. See
[Routers, services and middlewares](/proxy/routing).

## Step 5: Spread the load, or take a node out

If a node itself is struggling (high CPU, its own link full), there are two moves:

- **Add nodes.** Start dnz-proxy on another server with the connect key and approve it. DNS starts
  answering with its address too.
- **Drain a node.** It drops out of DNS answers but keeps serving the visitors already on it, so you
  can look at it, or restart it, without cutting anyone off:

```bash
dnz proxy nodes                 # every node, its state and addresses
dnz proxy drain jkt-1           # out of DNS answers, still serving
dnz proxy drain jkt-1 --undo    # back in
```

A name can also go through several proxies at once, say one in each building or data center. Each
proxy's healthy nodes are in the answer, so one site going down doesn't take the name with it.

## Step 6: The last resort, a status page

Sometimes the right call is to stop serving the site for a while: the origin needs a restart, or you
want the attack to lose interest. Don't let visitors stare at timeouts. Put up a page on every node
at once, and keep your own network let through:

```bash
dnz status-page set portal.example.com maintenance --for 1h --allow 10.0.0.0/8
```

Visitors get a clean **Down for maintenance** page with a 503 and a `Retry-After` counting down to the
end of the window, while your team on `10.0.0.0/8` still reaches the real site to check the fix.
Nothing behind the proxy is touched. When you're done:

```bash
dnz status-page off portal.example.com    # kept, ready for next time
```

See [Status pages](/proxy/status-pages) for the other statuses, such as `rate_limited` (429) and
`blocked` (403).

## After it's over

- **Turn the challenge off**, or narrow it, so ordinary visitors stop seeing it.
- **Keep the firewall rules** that blocked real attackers, and change the broad ones (a whole
  country) to **Log** so you'll see if they come back.
- **Purge the cache** if anything wrong got cached during the incident:

  ```bash
  dnz proxy purge "Edge JKT" --host portal.example.com
  ```

- **Read the audit log.** Every change made during the incident is in the
  [audit log](/admin/audit-log): who changed what, and when. It writes the post-mortem timeline for
  you.

## What this doesn't stop

dnz-proxy works on requests that reach it. A volumetric flood that fills your internet link
before it gets to any server can't be fixed by anything behind that link: no firewall rule helps when
the pipe itself is full. For that you need your upstream provider's filtering, or a network built to
absorb it in front of you.

Everything above the network layer, the floods of HTTP requests, the scrapers, the login attacks,
the one script someone forgot to rate limit, is where dnz-proxy does its job, on servers you own,
with every change live within seconds.
